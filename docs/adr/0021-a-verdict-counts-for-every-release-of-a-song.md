# A verdict counts for every release of a song

Spotify gives each release of a recording its own track id: the album track, its remaster, the deluxe edition and a best-of are four tracks. Verdicts are keyed by track id (ADR 0005), so a playlist holding two releases of a song showed it twice in needle's Playlist review, and rating one left the other unrated: the listener was asked about a song they had just rated, as if the rating had been lost. haystack already keeps each track's ISRC, which names the recording and is the same on every release of it.

So from 0.8.0 the API knows the **Song**. A track card carries `song`, its ISRC (API feature `card.song`), and a Song's verdict is the newest current verdict on any of its tracks. A playlist card's `verdict`, `playlists` and `liked`, the playlist snapshot's `verdicts`, and which liked songs wait in Rate your likes all go by the Song. needle lists a Song once in a Playlist review and skips it when any of its releases has a verdict.

Storage doesn't change. Each verdict stays a row on the track it was given to, and its Spotify writes (the heart, Discoveries, To Rip) go to that track only. The Song's verdict is worked out when it is read.

**Why not Discoveries and Calibrate.** An offer or a pick is closed by a verdict on its own track (`_close_offer`, `judge_picks`). If a Discoveries card showed a verdict given to another release, needle would treat the offer as reviewed while haystack still had it open, and the clear before the next batch would record it as a no. So those two queues keep the track's own verdict. A candidate is a song the listener hasn't judged, so the two rarely differ there.

**Considered Options**: storing one verdict per ISRC (a migration of the log, Spotify writes that would need a track chosen per Song, and nothing to go on for a track with no ISRC); matching releases by artist and title (a live take, a remix or a radio edit would count as the same song; the ISRC is exact); fixing it in needle alone (needle sees only the playlists it fetched, so a verdict on a release in another playlist, or one given before the cards were cached, would still be missed).

**Consequences**: a track with no ISRC is a Song of its own, as before. A remaster that was given a new ISRC is a different Song, so it can still be asked about again. Two releases can carry different current verdicts, from before 0.8.0 or from the CLI: the newest one is the Song's, but each track keeps its own Spotify state, so an older 3★ on one release still keeps that track's heart. The re-ranker and the Baseline still count each track's own verdict, so a song rated on two releases counts twice there. An older needle ignores `song`: it lists each release, but a Playlist review started after one of them is rated skips them all.

## Note (2026-10-23): the batch and Calibrate go by the Song too

The harvest matches a new candidate against the library and Liked Songs once, when it arrives, and the batch and the Calibrate top-up then checked Spotify ids only (`calibrate.known`). A song liked or ripped after its candidate arrived stayed in the pool, and its other releases carry other Spotify ids. On the reference installation, after about 500 ratings given in one week through Playlist review and Rate your likes, 68 pool candidates were the same song as a liked song or a library file, and under the variant the next batch would use, 13 of the top 15 by score were: the same audio as a positive scores about 0.99. Six of eight checked carried the liked track's ISRC, so whether the batch caught one depended on which release Spotify's ISRC search listed first. Calibrate was not affected, since it orders by uncertainty rather than score.

So `known.Known` matches each pool row against the library's files and the tracks the listener liked, judged, was offered or asked about: by ISRC where the candidate has one, else by the names. The names rule (`known.same_song`) wants the same artist (similarity 0.8 or more), no hard version marker between the titles, a length within 90 s, and the base titles equal once the version markers are stripped, or one the other plus a decoration that carries no version marker ('I Don't Want to Miss a Thing - From "Armageddon" Soundtrack', 'Happy - From "Despicable Me 2"'; not 'help_urself 2' or 'Wish You Were Here (Take 1)'). The batch and the Calibrate top-up skip what it matches (`known_songs` in the plan and the batch summary), and the hourly sync marks pool candidates that have become a liked song or a library file `excluded` with the harvest's reasons (`liked`, `in_library`). A judged song's other releases stay in the pool, skipped at selection: the verdict's state is on the track that got it. Storage is unchanged.

**Why not the harvest's own rule.** `matching.compare` within 3 s, the intake rule, caught 8 of the 68 on the reference installation: Deezer's length for the same recording differs from Spotify's or the file's by 4 to 30 s often enough (Hells Bells 309 against 313, Gotye 241 against 245, a 2009 remaster 310 against 280), and Deezer lists 30 s, the preview's length, for a track it has only the preview of. With the length freed to 90 s, compare's fuzzy title test paired different songs that share a suffix (FATE with Faded, Panama (2015 Remaster) with Jump - 2015 Remaster, Bangarang (feat. Sirah) with Kyoto (feat. Sirah)): 70 library matches, about 30 of them wrong. Exact base titles with the decoration rule matched 37 library files and 7 liked songs, every one checked by hand the same song, plus 43 judged tracks by ISRC.

**Considered Options**: the harvest's intake rule (above); matching by song key alone, artist and base title, as the eval's pool filter does (it would drop every remix of a liked song, and those are discoveries in their own right); the check at the batch only (Calibrate and the pool's counts would keep the duplicates); excluding a judged song's other releases from the pool too (a verdict is given to one track and `_close_offer` moves that track only; the skip at selection is enough).
